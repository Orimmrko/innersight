InnerSight · Stage 1 · study notes

Stage 1, in plain words

What we built, why we built it that way, and what we have now. Written to be read once and remembered, not to look impressive.

1The problem

An insider is an employee who already has permission to do everything they do.

They don't hack in. They don't steal a password. They log in with their own account, open files they're allowed to open, and copy them to a USB stick they're allowed to use. Then they walk out.

This is why normal security tools fail. Those tools look for things that are not allowed: a stranger's IP address, a known virus, a blocked website. An insider does nothing that isn't allowed.

So we changed the question.

Instead of asking "is this action bad?" — which has no useful answer — we ask "is this normal for this specific person?"

Here is why that works. Suppose someone connects a USB stick five times today.

  • If they're a hardware engineer who does that every day — nothing happened.
  • If they're an accountant who has never touched a USB stick in eighteen months — something happened.

Same action. Same number. Completely different meaning. The only thing that tells them apart is knowing what each person normally does.

So the system needs a picture of "normal" for every single employee. Building that picture is what Stage 1 is for.

2What Stage 1 actually does

The raw data is five big files. Every login, every USB plug-in, every file copy, every email, every website visit — for 1,000 employees over 17 months. About 16 gigabytes.

That's 32.8 million separate events. Far too much to work with, and most of it is noise.

Stage 1 squeezes it down to 18 numbers per person per day.

What Stage 1 does NOT do

It doesn't mark anyone as guilty. It doesn't calculate anything clever. It doesn't train a model. It just counts.

That's on purpose. Counting is the slow part, so we keep the slow part as simple as possible. Everything that might change later — how we mark attacks, how we measure "unusual" — lives in later stages, which run in seconds.

3Why we used this dataset

This is the first question you'll be asked, so it's worth knowing the answer cold.

Real companies do not publish their employees' activity logs. So there is one standard research dataset, made by Carnegie Mellon University: a simulated company with fake employees, where some of them are secretly attackers and we're told exactly who.

It comes in ten versions. We counted the number of known attackers in each one:

Eight of the ten versions have almost no attackers in them. r4.1 has three people. You can't teach a model from three examples, and you certainly can't split three people into a training group and a testing group.

That leaves two usable versions. r5.2 is twice as big and twice as complicated. So:

Say this

“r4.2 is the smallest dataset in the whole collection that actually has enough labelled attackers to train on. That's why it's the standard benchmark, and that's why we used it.”

Admit this before someone points it out. 70 attackers out of 1,000 employees is 7%. In a real company it's nowhere near that — it's closer to zero. The dataset's own documentation calls this an “unrealistically high amount” of attack data, on purpose, so researchers have something to work with. We used it because it's the only option, not because it's realistic. Saying that yourself sounds like you understand your data. Being caught by it sounds like you didn't check.

Two details worth knowing:

"Outside work hours" means before 7am or after 7pm. We didn't pick those hours — they come from the dataset's own documentation. That matters, because if we'd tuned them ourselves someone could say we picked whatever made our results look best.

Email counts check the "bcc" field. An employee can send an email to a colleague and secretly blind-copy it to their personal Gmail. If you only look at the "to" field, that looks like a completely internal email. Checking bcc catches it — and that's exactly the trick attack type 2 uses.

5Three of the 18 don't work — and we say so

Look at rows 8, 9 and 10 in that table. 445,581 three times. And row 13 is zero.

Those aren't bugs. Here's what happened:

NumberProblemWhy
files copied to USBsame as "files copied"In this dataset every file copy is already a copy to USB. There's nothing else to count.
different filenamessame as "files copied"The fake data gives every file a random new name, so 445,581 files have 445,581 different names.

Say this

“All 18 definitions are correct, and all three of these work fine on the newer versions of the dataset. On this one specific version they happen to be useless. So r4.2 really gives us 15 useful numbers, not 18 — and we found that by measuring, not by assuming.”

Say this before anyone asks. If an examiner spots it first, it looks like a mistake you missed. If you raise it, it looks like you know your data.

6The two lists we had to choose

Sixteen of the eighteen numbers are just counting — no opinions involved. Two of them are different.

"Visits to job websites" and "visits to file-upload websites" require deciding which websites count. That's the only place in Stage 1 where we could have quietly cheated. So we decided both by measuring.

The test we used

Do the attackers visit this website during their attack, and not otherwise?

For job sites, we checked two known attackers. Here's how many times each visited each site inside their attack period versus outside it:

Websiteduring / outsideDecision
monster.com10 / 0    7 / 0keep
careerbuilder.com11 / 0   10 / 0keep
indeed.com3 / 0     4 / 0keep
aol.com/jobs5 / 0     5 / 0added — behaves the same
craigslist.org17 / 22dropped — more visits outside than inside
linkedin.com40,446 visits by everyonedropped — it's just normal browsing

The mistake we caught, and this one is the good story

The upload-website list started with four sites. One of them, mediafire.com, turned out to be visited about 334,000 times — by basically everyone, all the time. It's a popular file-sharing site.

Meanwhile the site that actually matters, wikileaks.org, appears about 90 times in 28 million web requests.

So that one number was 90% "browsed a normal website" and 0.03% "uploaded stolen data".

Why this was worse than it sounds. Because we measure each person against their own history, the damage wasn't spread evenly — it hit some attackers and not others. One attacker never used mediafire, so his normal was zero and his leak-site visit stood out enormously. Another attacker browsed mediafire regularly, so his normal was already noisy and the same visit vanished into it. Whether we caught an attacker depended on their unrelated browsing habits. That's a coin flip.

We removed mediafire. Here's what happened to the attacker whose signal it was hiding:

If someone says “isn't dropping websites cheating?”

“The candidate websites came from the dataset's own published descriptions of the attacks — that's what every paper on this dataset does. What we removed was decided by one test applied to every candidate equally: does this site get visited during attacks and not otherwise? Craigslist and mediafire failed that test clearly. And we deliberately kept two upload sites that have nothing to do with any known attack, so the number isn't just a lookup for the one website the answer key mentions.”

7How we know it's actually correct

Checking that the program didn't crash proves nothing. Checking the row counts proves almost nothing. Here's the test that actually proves it.

Take attacker KPC0073. In seventeen months, his entire history contains 16 USB events. Here's what our program produced for him:

       day    logins  after-hours  usb  job sites  upload sites
2010-02-16         1            0    0         14             0
2010-03-22         1            0    0         11             0
2010-06-08         1            0    0          3             0
2010-07-06         2            1    2          0             0
2010-07-07         2            1    1          0             1
2010-07-09         2            1    3          0             0
2010-07-12         2            1    1          0             1
2010-07-15         2            1    1          0             1
(the other 127 days: nothing at all)

Now here's what the dataset's answer key says he did:

07/07/2010 21:23   visited wikileaks.org
07/12/2010 23:51   visited wikileaks.org
07/15/2010 02:40   visited wikileaks.org
They match exactly. Our "upload sites" column shows 1 on 07-07, 07-12 and 07-15 — the same three days the answer key lists — and 0 on all 498 other days. His USB activity shows up on exactly the five days the raw logs contain it, and never before July. His logins go from 1 to 2 and his after-hours logins go from 0 to 1 on those same days.

Look at that table again, because it's also the clearest possible example of the whole idea:

Nothing in those rows is against the rules. Logging in is fine. Using a USB stick is fine. Visiting a website is fine. The only thing that's strange is that he did them, when he never had before.

The other checks

  • 9 automated tests that run in half a second.
  • Checks printed every single time the program runs — no negative counts, no missing values, no duplicated days.
  • Three "fingerprint" checks confirming the known quirks are still there. If "big attachments" ever stops being zero, we're not looking at r4.2 any more.
  • We confirmed r4.1 and r4.2 share zero out of 1,000 usernames, so the two versions can't be mixed up by accident.

8Why it only takes 90 seconds

People are surprised that 16 GB processes in a minute and a half. Two reasons:

Most of those 16 GB is junk we skip. Every log file has a "content" column full of generated filler text. We don't use it for anything, so we tell the file reader to skip that column entirely. It never gets loaded into memory at all.

We never load a whole file. The biggest file is read in slices of 50,000 rows. Each slice is immediately reduced to daily counts and then thrown away. So the memory used depends on how many people and how many days — never on how big the file is.

Why 330,452 and not 501,000? 1,000 people × 501 days would be 501,000. But Stage 1 only writes a line for a day where something actually happened — and most people do nothing at weekends. About 358 of the 501 days are weekdays, and some employees left partway through. Stage 2 fills the missing days back in with zeros, because a day where someone did nothing is still information — especially for an attacker who has resigned and stopped coming to work.

10Weaknesses to admit up front

  • The data is fake and deliberately overloaded with attackers. 7% here, near-zero in reality.
  • Only 15 of the 18 numbers are useful on this version. Correct definitions, useless data.
  • The answer key is narrower than the actual bad behaviour. Attacker BIH0745's official attack window is 65 minutes long — but his heaviest late-night USB copying happened outside it. So our model gets marked wrong for spotting real suspicious behaviour. That's the dataset's problem, not ours, and we don't try to "fix" it by widening the windows — that would be adjusting the answers to suit the model.
  • Our website lists come from knowing what the attacks were. Standard practice for this dataset, but a real security team wouldn't have that on day one.
  • Part of the code was reused from an earlier version of this project. The original is kept untouched in extraction/salvage/provenance/, and everything it claimed was re-checked against the real data.

11Questions you'll get

Why fake data instead of real data?

Real insider-threat data doesn't exist publicly. No company publishes logs of their employees' activity, and the attackers are rare and legally sensitive. This Carnegie Mellon dataset is the standard because it's the only labelled one that exists. Within it, only two of ten versions have enough attackers to train on, and we picked the smaller one.

Why one line per person per day?

One line per event is too detailed — a single login tells you nothing, and the answer key marks periods, not individual clicks. One line per month is too coarse — attack type 1 lasts as little as one day and would disappear entirely. A day matches how the answers are written, keeps all 70 attackers, and still lets the second model look at 28 days in a row by stacking lines.

How do you know your counting is right?

Three ways. Automated tests cover the logic. Automatic checks run every time the program runs. And most convincingly, we rebuilt a known attacker's timeline and it matched the official answer key day for day — including the three leak-site visits on exactly the dates the answer key lists.

Three of your numbers are broken. Isn't that a bug?

No — the definitions are right, and they work on the newer versions of the dataset. On this version the data makes them useless: there's no separate "copied to USB" column because every file copy already is one, every filename is randomly unique, and no email is big enough to hit our attachment cutoff. We measured all three rather than assuming, and we report 15 useful numbers instead of claiming 18.

Why does 16 GB only take 90 seconds?

Because most of it is a filler column we skip before it's ever loaded, and we read the files in slices rather than all at once. The data is big but very thin — a lot of bytes carrying very little information.

The sentence to finish on.

“Stage 1 turns 32.8 million events into 330,452 lines of 18 daily counts, in 90 seconds, and we proved it's correct by rebuilding a known attacker's timeline and matching the official answer key exactly. It contains no model and no answers — it exists so that every question after it becomes a question about how unusual this is for this particular person, which is the only way insider threat is detectable at all.”
